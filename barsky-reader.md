I have lived my whole life suspicious of patterns: patterns of money and power, patterns of racism and sexism, patterns of exploitation of people and nature. And now a new thing comes into my life that scrambles the pattern: it looks like town officials colluded with a developer to approve an unusually massive project outside the boundaries they set out for themselves in the Master Plan, and never bothered to tell my neighborhood about it. They took away my sun by allowing a 45-foot wall on the property line. But to hear city officials tell the story, this is all about affordable housing, addressing structural racism, creating a more diverse, equitable, and inclusive Princeton.

Obviously that raises the question: Am I the asshole? I am sure some of you have an answer already!

But then I started thinking about the developer: Barsky. Is he asking himself this question? It seems lopsided to me that I should use this opportunity for self-reflection if no one else is.

So I did some research to better understand one of the people on the other side of this conflict. This is what I found. The TLDR is that this is not about affordable housing, addressing structural racism, creating a more diverse, equitable, and inclusive Princeton. It is about a developer who has spent twenty years pushing Princeton's planning process to its limits, and a planning process that has spent twenty years letting him.

The developer proposing to transform 86–94 Spruce Street into a 30-unit apartment complex is not new to Princeton, and not new to controversy. Roman Barsky and his son Daniel have been building in this town since 1995. Over that time, a consistent pattern has emerged: proposals that push to or beyond the legal limit, characterization of neighbor opposition as irrational or "silly," and a municipal planning apparatus that has repeatedly created the zoning instruments enabling their projects.

What follows is drawn from contemporaneous news reporting. It is not an argument against development. It is a record.

---

## I. Barsky Court, Nassau Street — *c. 2000–2004*

Roman Barsky proposed five houses on Nassau Street behind the landmark "Captain's House," adjacent to the historic Queenston Place neighborhood. The fight lasted four years.

In a 2007 profile by Kathleen McGinn Spring in the *Community News*, Roman Barsky described his experience with neighbor opposition across his career:

> "You have to do it with almost every project in Princeton."

On the Barsky Court fight specifically, the article notes that residents "complained that the proposed houses — taking up about half of their approximately 10,000-square-foot lots — were too large, too boxy, and out of character with the neighborhood."

Barsky prevailed. He moved into one of the finished Barsky Court homes himself. On the prospect of a chilly reception from his new neighbors — recently his adversaries — he told the reporter:

> "I'm friendly with everybody. I have no enemies."

The article's summary of his broader attitude: "He finds the outcry over this project silly. He finds the outcry over many of his other projects silly, too."

## II. "Jane's House," 60 South Harrison Street — *2005*

This conflict reached the *New York Times*. Roman and Igor Barsky purchased 60 South Harrison Street — a 1928 craftsman colonial in the historic Jugtown neighborhood, home to Jane Taylor for 49 years — outbidding neighbors who had offered over asking price. The plan was to demolish the house and build two larger ones in its place.

Neighbors Kathleen Abplanalp and Charles Douglas mobilized the neighborhood. "The character of our neighborhood," Douglas told the *Times*, "is under assault by these developers who look for any larger lot on which to build."

The Barskys pushed back. Roman:

> "What is upsetting us is the neighbors are saying it is like we will tear down anything to make a dollar. Yes, we make a living at this. But we live in this town; we love this town."

Igor, on the house itself:

> "Then, all of a sudden, this house that has been neglected, not painted in 40 or 50 years, becomes this wonderful building that must be saved."

Igor, on the neighborhood campaign:

> "All this complaining is worth nothing."

Even the elderly homeowner, Jane Taylor — who had not previously spoken publicly — ultimately told the *Times*:

> "I love the place. I raised three children here. The idea of it being torn down is distressing, and they're going to take out the woods in back, and the privet hedges. I think it will be a disappointment to the whole neighborhood, and I just don't think it's right."

A Borough Council member, Roger Martindell, pushed for a quick moratorium on demolitions. The mayor blocked it; the town's lawyer said such a moratorium would be illegal. The community group "Save Jane's House" was unable to stop the demolition.

## III. 31–33 Lytle Street — *2014–2015*

Roman Barsky obtained demolition permits for a historic property at 31–33 Lytle Street in the Witherspoon-Jackson neighborhood, adjacent to Mary Moss Park. Mayor Lempert's office reached out to ask whether he would be willing to sell to the municipality instead. According to reporting in *Planet Princeton*, Barsky did not respond to requests for comment.

The municipality ultimately purchased the property using Open Space funds to prevent the demolition — not because Barsky was legally stopped, but because the town bought him out.

A reader commenting on *Planet Princeton*'s coverage wrote: "Roman Barsky seems to be redesigning Princeton one bland McMansion at a time."

## IV. 344 Nassau Street / The Hornor House, Jugtown — *2023–2025*

The most recent and extensively documented conflict involves Daniel Barsky and the 18th-century Joseph Hornor House at the corner of Nassau and Harrison Streets — described by the Jugtown Historic District's National Register nomination as "the single most important and pivotal building at the Jugtown crossroads."

Daniel proposed a 45-foot, four-story addition that would have required demolishing the rear of the Hornor House and engulfed it in a new apartment structure. The application was made possible by the Affordable Housing Overlay ordinance (AHO-2) that Princeton Council adopted in 2020 — critics noted, without notice to neighbors or residents.

Neighbors organized as Save Jugtown, retaining attorney Bruce Afran and historians Clifford Zink and David Kinsey. In May 2024, the Historic Preservation Commission unanimously denied the application. Reporting in *Planet Princeton* noted the HPC found the plan clearly failed to meet historic preservation standards.

After rejection, Daniel Barsky negotiated directly with Save Jugtown. The redesigned proposal — a separate three-story building with 10 units rather than the original 15–20, with the Hornor House fully intact — was endorsed by the HPC in December 2025.

The *Town Topics* account of the compromise hearing quotes Barsky: "I'm really excited that we're finally here." Save Jugtown attorney Bruce Afran said that after the original rejection, "both sides immediately reached out, really demonstrating what planning is about when it works."

## The Pattern

Across four documented conflicts spanning twenty years, several elements recur:

**The proposals consistently push to or beyond the legal maximum.** Each project has required zoning variances, overlay ordinances, or other departures from as-of-right development. The Spruce Street project is no different: it required a custom zoning district, AH-12, created by a single ordinance, for a single parcel, at a density of roughly 64 units per acre — more than three times the maximum contemplated in Princeton's own 2023 Master Plan for the area.

**Neighbor opposition is characterized as irrational.** "Silly." "Worth nothing." This is not the language of a developer who views neighbors as stakeholders.

**The municipality has been the enabling mechanism.** The Barsky Court variances, the AHO-2 overlay that enabled the Hornor House application, and now the AH-12 ordinance for Spruce Street: each project has required the municipality to create or bend a rule. In each case, it did.

**Legal opposition forces redesign.** When neighbors organized with legal representation and engaged the actual regulatory framework — as happened at Jugtown — the project was scaled back significantly. When they could not, the project proceeded.

The residents of Spruce Street, Hamilton Avenue, and the surrounding neighborhood are not the first people to find themselves in this position.
