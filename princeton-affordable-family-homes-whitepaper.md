# Counting Doors, Not Homes

### How Princeton's Fourth Round Housing Plan Meets Its Numbers While Missing Affordable Families

*A white paper on the unit-size mix in Princeton's 2025 Housing Element and Fair Share Plan and its February 2026 implementing ordinances.*

---

## Summary

Princeton has adopted a Fourth Round Housing Element and Fair Share Plan (HEFSP) that satisfies its legal affordable-housing obligation by the numbers and was approved by the Superior Court in February 2026. That is a real achievement, and nothing in this paper disputes the town's obligation or its right to grow.

But meeting an obligation counted in *units* is not the same as meeting the need it exists to serve. The deepest shortage—locally and nationally—is in **affordable homes large enough for families**: two- and three-bedroom units. Princeton's plan and its implementing ordinances satisfy the unit *count* and the income split, but they make almost no commitment to the unit *size mix*. They defer bedroom distribution to a state rule that can be satisfied in the aggregate, and they place no family-size requirement at all on the market-rate units that make up the large majority of what actually gets built.

The clearest illustration is the town's own land. On three municipally owned sites—the Chestnut Street firehouse, the Harrison Street firehouse and garage, and the 303 John Street public-works depot—Princeton plans **85 units of 100% affordable family rental housing, financed with public money, on land it controls outright.** Even here, where there is no developer profit motive and no market-rate component to balance, neither the plan nor the zoning commits to a single family-sized unit. If the city will not build family homes where it has total control, it cedes the family gap to private developers—who are structurally incentivized to build the smallest units they can. The result is visible at 86–88 and 92–94 Spruce Street, where the approved plan packs roughly 600-square-foot apartments at 64 units per acre with 17 parking spaces and no usable open space.

This paper documents the gap and proposes a constructive fix: **commit to a family bedroom mix where the city has the most control, and write that commitment into the ordinances rather than leaving it to chance.**

---

## 1. The obligation, and how Princeton is meeting it

Under the Mount Laurel doctrine and the 2024 amendments to the Fair Housing Act (P.L. 2024 c.2), every New Jersey municipality must provide a realistic opportunity for its fair share of regional affordable-housing need. For the Fourth Round (2025–2035), Princeton accepted a Present Need (rehabilitation) component of 60 units and a Prospective Need of 276 units, and adopted a plan spread across more than a dozen sites that, with prior-round credits, the town and the Court found fully satisfies that obligation. (Fourth Round HEFSP, pp. 11–12, 78–80.)

The mechanism is overwhelmingly the standard inclusionary model: developments are required to set aside roughly 20% of units as deed-restricted affordable, with the remaining ~80% rented or sold at market rate. The plan projects that Princeton may see **as many as 920 new dwellings by 2035** (roughly 550 units of "organic" growth plus the plan's developments). (HEFSP, p. 22.)

That arithmetic matters: the affordable obligation is a few hundred units, but the housing actually built will number close to a thousand. **The vast majority of new homes in Princeton over the next decade will be market-rate—and, as Section 4 explains, market-rate units carry no size requirement whatsoever.**

---

## 2. The gap that matters: family-sized affordable homes

National research is consistent on where the affordable shortage is most acute. The Harvard Joint Center for Housing Studies, measuring the supply of family-friendly rentals for households with children, found the deepest deficits among the lowest-income families and identified **two-bedroom homes as the single largest shortfall**, with a gap on the order of 1.4 million units nationally. Analyses of recent construction (for example, the Bay Area Equity Atlas) show the opposite of what's needed: a majority of rentals built since 2010 are studios or one-bedrooms, while three-or-more-bedroom apartments make up only about 15% of new construction—accompanied by rising overcrowding concentrated among low-income families.

Princeton's own Master Plan names the problem, repeatedly invoking the "missing middle." But "missing middle" has two different meanings that are easy to blur. One is a *building form*—duplexes, townhouses, small multi-unit buildings at a neighborhood scale. The other is a *price point* between subsidized and luxury housing. A development can satisfy the form definition (small, modestly scaled units) while doing nothing for families, because nothing about "missing middle" requires the bedrooms a family needs. New Jersey's affordable-housing rules recognize this directly: they require affordable *family* units to be built at family sizes (see Section 4).

---

## 3. Where the plan goes quiet: the bedroom mix

New Jersey's Uniform Housing Affordability Controls (UHAC), at N.J.A.C. 5:80-26.4(e), set bedroom-distribution standards for affordable *family* (non-age-restricted) units. Across those units:

- the average must be at least **two bedrooms per unit**;
- at least **50%** must be two- or three-bedroom;
- no more than **20%** may be efficiencies or one-bedrooms;
- at least **30%** must be two-bedroom; and
- at least **20%** must be three-bedroom.

These are meaningful family-size floors. But two features of the rule, combined with how Princeton's plan is written, drain them of force in practice.

**First, the rule is measured in the aggregate.** Subsection (l) provides that the bedroom-distribution requirements must be satisfied by all restricted units in the municipality *considered together*, and that any individual development's mix may be waived or altered with state approval so long as it does not materially deviate from the housing plan. So no single project—including any of the sites discussed below—has to demonstrate a family mix on its own. The town can lean on family units built years ago to "cover" small units built today.

**Second, the rule governs only the affordable units.** It does not touch the ~80% of inclusionary units that are market-rate, nor the ~550 units of organic growth. Princeton justifies smaller units by pointing to a "mismatch" in its own data—71.4% of households have three or fewer people, but only 37.3% of homes are efficiencies, one- or two-bedrooms (HEFSP, p. 21). But that statistic describes *market demand and preference* among households who can pay—Princeton's median household income is $184,113 and its median family income exceeds $250,000 (HEFSP, p. 21). It says nothing about the affordable-family need the Mount Laurel program exists to serve.

Against this backdrop, the plan's actual commitment to family-sized units is remarkably thin. In 272 pages, the **only explicit Fourth Round bedroom commitment** is that, of 33 family very-low-income units, at least 16 will have two or more bedrooms (HEFSP, p. 80)—a requirement that flows from a separate "families with children" provision of the 2024 law, not from any town initiative. Everything else is deferred to UHAC at the site-plan stage.

---

## 4. The clearest test: the city's own three sites

If Princeton genuinely prioritized affordable family homes, the place to prove it is on the land the city owns, develops with public money, and reserves entirely for affordable housing—where there is no market-rate component and no developer margin standing in the way. The Fourth Round plan includes three such sites, all designated **100% affordable family rental**:

| Site | Units | Income split (VL / Low / Mod) | Owner / developer |
|---|---|---|---|
| Chestnut Street firehouse (13 Chestnut St) | 16 | 2 / 6 / 8 | City → Princeton Community Housing |
| Harrison Street firehouse & garage (27 N. Harrison St) | 34 | 5 / 12 / 17 | City → qualified developer |
| 303 John Street (DPW depot) | 35 | 5 / 13 / 17 | City → Princeton Community Housing |
| **Total** | **85** | **12 / 31 / 42** | |

*(HEFSP, pp. 71–73, Table 29.)*

Every one of these is described in the plan as an "affordable family rental complex." Yet:

- **The plan commits to no bedroom mix for any of them.** It states unit counts and income levels, and nothing about how many bedrooms those units will have.
- **The implementing zoning is silent too.** The Chestnut Street ordinance (AH-16, adopted February 2026) requires that all dwellings comply with UHAC and sets a 16-unit minimum, four stories / 48 feet, 90% maximum impervious coverage, and **0.5 parking spaces per unit**—but specifies no family bedroom requirement. A parking ratio of one space per two units is difficult to reconcile with family occupancy, where two cars per household is common; it is a design signal that small units are anticipated.

These are 85 affordable family homes that the public is paying to build, on public land, with no enforceable commitment that they will be sized for families. That is the cleanest possible evidence that the unit-size mix has slipped out of view: **if the city will not commit to family homes here, it is unlikely to demand them of private developers who have every incentive to build smaller—and the gap those city sites were meant to close gets handed downstream to the inclusionary market, where it will not be filled.**

---

## 5. Exhibit A: 86–88 & 92–94 Spruce Street (AH-12)

The downstream result is already on the table. The AH-12 ordinance (adopted February 2026) rezoned two adjacent Spruce Street lots—about 0.66 acre that already hold a 10-unit apartment building and a duplex—to permit **30 additional units, for 42 total: roughly 64 units per acre.** The approved concept ("Spruce Path," dated May 30, 2025) shows the 30 new units as predominantly ~600-square-foot apartments, with the largest building's parking tucked underneath, **17 parking spaces** for the whole site, and no programmed open or green space (the ordinance permits 75% impervious coverage).

The ordinance requires a minimum of 8 affordable units (20%, with a floor of 8) and, like AH-16, simply defers their sizing to UHAC. It sets parking at **0.425 spaces per unit**. There is no requirement that any unit—affordable or market-rate—be sized for a family. Spruce Street is what "compliant" looks like when the size mix is left to the developer: a high count of very small units, minimal parking, and no family housing.

*(Note: the concept plan labels a strip along the western edge of the site "Green Acres." If that denotes State Green Acres–encumbered open space, its development would trigger a separate and demanding state diversion process; the designation is worth confirming on the record.)*

## 6. "Smaller homes" is not "affordable family homes"

The strongest objection to this critique is the abundance argument: build more housing of any size, and pressure eases across the whole market. There is real evidence that supply constraints raise prices—the research behind reforms like Massachusetts' 2026 "Legalize Starter Homes" ballot question (which would allow modest single-family homes on smaller lots) draws on decades of work showing that restrictive minimum-lot-size zoning inflates prices and suppresses supply. Supply matters, and this paper does not argue otherwise.

But supply of the *wrong kind* does not close the *right gap*. Princeton has added roughly 1,500 apartments over the past decade, and its median one-bedroom rent still climbed to about $2,751—small units did not become affordable simply by being small. Deed-restricted affordable units solve the price problem by regulation; the question this paper raises is only about their *size*. For the affordable units the public is mandating and often funding, sizing them for one- and two-person households when the documented shortage is for families is a missed opportunity that more small units cannot fix.

---

## 7. A positive plan: what Princeton should commit to

None of the following requires reopening the court-approved plan. Each can be accomplished through the implementing ordinances, developer agreements, and site-plan approvals that are still being written.

1. **Set a real family bedroom mix on the three city-owned sites.** Because the Chestnut Street, Harrison Street, and 303 John Street developments are 100% affordable, city-owned, and publicly financed, the municipality controls their unit mix completely. The conveyance and developer agreements should require a defined family mix—for example, a majority of units at two or more bedrooms, a three-bedroom share well above the 20% UHAC floor, and a hard cap on efficiencies and one-bedrooms—so that 85 public family units are actually built for families.

2. **Write bedroom standards into the AH ordinances.** Rather than deferring sizing to UHAC aggregation, the AH-12, AH-16, and comparable ordinances should state their own minimum family mix on the face of the zoning, so that compliance is demonstrated project by project rather than presumed town-wide.

3. **Require a family share among market-rate units, too.** The size problem is largest in the unregulated 80%. The town can condition AH and inclusionary zoning on a minimum proportion of two- and three-bedroom homes across *all* units, not just the affordable set-aside.

4. **Publish the aggregate math.** Princeton should produce and post the projected town-wide Fourth Round affordable bedroom distribution and demonstrate that it clears the 30% two-bedroom and 20% three-bedroom floors—rather than relying silently on the aggregation provision. If it cannot show this, that is itself the finding.

5. **Make the sites livable for families.** Parking and open-space standards (0.425–0.5 spaces per unit, 75–90% impervious coverage, no programmed green space) are calibrated for small-household occupancy. Family-oriented sites need parking and outdoor space to match.

6. **For Spruce Street specifically:** require a family bedroom mix, restore adequate parking, provide usable open space, and confirm the status of the "Green Acres" strip before approval.

---

## 8. A fair reading

Three caveats, in the interest of credibility:

- The plan does repeatedly designate the city sites as *family* rentals, and UHAC's family bedroom floors will apply when each development is approved. The concern is the absence of a *specific commitment* and the design signals pointing the other way—not a claim that the town is legally building studios.
- Princeton's existing affordable stock includes genuine family units (three-bedroom for-sale homes, three-bedroom group-home apartments). The town may argue its *cumulative* record satisfies the family floors. That is exactly why the aggregate numbers in Recommendation 4 should be put on the public record.
- This paper describes the regulatory framework and the public record; it is not legal advice. The aggregation argument under N.J.A.C. 5:80-26.4(l) should be reviewed by an affordable-housing attorney before it is relied upon.

The ask is simple and constructive: **count homes families can live in, not just doors.** Princeton has met its number. It should now make sure the homes behind that number include the families the affordable-housing obligation was created to serve.
