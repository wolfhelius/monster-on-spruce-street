# A History of Princeton Teardowns

*Compiled for the Monster on Spruce Street project*

---

## Introduction

The residential teardown — the purchase of a modest older home, its demolition, and replacement with a much larger and more expensive new structure — has been a defining source of community anxiety in Princeton, New Jersey for at least two decades. What follows is a documented timeline of that anxiety, drawn from *New York Times* and *Town Topics* reporting, Princeton Community News, and the municipality's own official planning documents. The record shows a problem identified as early as 2005, officially acknowledged by the Planning Board in 2007, declared a civic crisis in 2016, partially addressed by ordinance in 2018, and still unresolved as of the 2023 Master Plan.

The Barsky family — Roman Barsky and his brother Igor, later joined by Roman's son Daniel — appear by name at the very beginning of this documented record and remain central to it throughout.

---

## 2005: "Jane's House" and the First Public Reckoning

The earliest documented flash point appears in the *New York Times* on September 25, 2005, in a piece titled ["Progress? Or Tearing Down History?"](https://www.nytimes.com/2005/09/25/realestate/progress-or-tearing-down-history.html) The article centers on 60 South Harrison Street in Princeton's historic Jugtown neighborhood, where 80-year-old Jane C. Taylor had lived for 49 years. Her craftsman colonial — described by a neighbor as "the finest house on the street" — had been sold to **Roman and Igor Barsky**, who filed plans to demolish it and build two larger homes on the 0.3-acre property.

Neighbor Kathleen Abplanalp, who had made an above-asking offer on the house only to lose it to the Barskys, told the *Times* that three houses had already been torn down on her street that year. She and her husband Charles Douglas rallied dozens of neighbors and organized a group called **Save Jane's House**, appearing before Borough Council to demand a moratorium on teardowns. The town's lawyer ruled such a moratorium would be illegal.

Princeton's mayor at the time, Joseph O'Neill, had already been working on an "anti-McMansion" ordinance since earlier that spring — inspired, he said, by a summer he had spent in Palo Alto, California, where 300 teardowns had occurred in a single year. He noted that only "about a half dozen" Princeton houses had been torn down that year, but was anticipating the trend.

Igor Barsky dismissed community opposition: *"All this complaining is worth nothing."* Roman Barsky framed their work differently: *"We live in this town; we love this town. We have children going to school here."*

The *Times* article is the earliest documented instance of the Barsky name appearing in the public record in direct connection with the teardown controversy.

## 2007: Official Acknowledgment — The First Master Plan Reexamination

Princeton's Planning Board completed its second reexamination of its 1996 Master Plan in 2007. This document represents the **first official, formal recognition** of the teardown problem in Princeton's planning record. The 2007 Reexamination:

- Identified "the increasing number of teardowns occurring in certain neighborhoods as a growing problem"
- Noted that small and modest homes were being demolished and replaced by larger, more expensive ones
- Warned that this trend "both affects the visible character of our neighborhoods and impacts affordability"
- Recommended "developing new zoning standards to address neighborhood character"
- Called for "neighborhood conservation districts" and new controls on height, setback, and bulk

These recommendations sat largely unimplemented for nearly a decade.

Also in 2007, the Princeton Community News profiled Roman Barsky in a lengthy feature: ["Roman Barsky's Long Road: From Russia to Real Estate"](https://www.communitynews.org/princetoninfo/coverstories/roman-barsky-s-long-road-from-russia-to-real-estate/article_8e75fe8c-6905-5d98-b25f-9363c2ba7fc2.html). The article describes a builder who had started in Princeton in 1995, who "is always looking for undersized houses on desirable streets to buy, tear down, and replace with a larger, up-to-date house." The article also documents Barsky Court — a four-year zoning battle over five houses built on Nassau Street behind the landmark "Captain's House" — as evidence of the friction his projects routinely generated. Barsky's formula at the time: buy a teardown for approximately $500,000, sell the replacement for approximately $1.5 million.

## 2013–2016: The Tsunami Builds

After the 2008–2010 recession temporarily cooled the market, the teardown pace accelerated sharply. The town's own 2017 Neighborhood Character and Zoning Initiative Progress Report would later document the scale: **83 homes were demolished and replaced by developers in Princeton between 2013 and 2016**, compared to 54 by private homeowners. In 2015, more than 40 building permits were issued for new single-family construction; in 2016, still 31.

The economics were stark. The median price of a new house in Princeton floated between **$1.3 and $1.5 million**; for an older home, $550,000–$630,000. This gap of $700,000–$900,000 provided the engine.

The [2014 Princeton Community Master Plan Reexamination](https://www.princetonnj.gov/DocumentCenter/View/841/Reexamination-Report-PDF) had continued to flag the issue, but zoning remedies remained elusive.

By 2014, the teardown phenomenon had even entered Princeton's literary culture. [Town Topics reported](https://www.towntopics.com/wordpress/2014/04/16/labyrinth-books-hosts-reading-of-keeleys-new-novel-about-princetons-megabuilders/) that Labyrinth Books hosted a reading of a novel set in Princeton whose premise centered on "ambitious megabuilders roaming the neighborhoods in search of modest postwar houses to tear down and replace with McMansions, forcing out the community's middle-class residents." The novel's plot — in which a retiring couple fights a father-and-son builder planning a McMansion next door and trying to induce them to sell their own home as a teardown — required no great imaginative leap for Princeton readers.

## 2016: The Political Crisis

The pace of demolitions in 2016 made teardowns the dominant issue in Princeton local politics — arguably the most heated civic debate the town had seen in years.

[*Town Topics* declared](https://www.towntopics.com/wordpress/2016/12/28/changing-character-of-princeton-defines-2016/) that "the rapid pace of teardowns and the often out-of-scale houses that replace them was an issue that dominated discussions in Princeton throughout 2016. The town's changing character was the theme in the platforms of **nearly every candidate** who ran for local office in 2016."

Key events of 2016:

- **April:** Princeton Council voted unanimously to hire a consultant — the RBA Group, which had done similar work in Haddonfield — to study residential zoning. [Town Topics reported](http://www.towntopics.com/wordpress/category/news/breaking-news/page/5/) that "the priority of the Neighborhood Character and Zoning Study will be the issue of teardowns, which are taking place in just about every area of the town."

- **June:** The town [launched a dedicated website](http://www.towntopics.com/wordpress/2016/06/29/town-launches-website-for-neighborhood-study/), princetonneighborhoods.org, to gather public input and track progress, "in response to citizens' concerns about residential development and the changing character of the town."

- **May (Letter to Editor):** An architect [argued in *Town Topics*](https://www.towntopics.com/wordpress/2016/05/11/princeton-need-not-waste-time-money-rewriting-zoning-to-stop-tear-downs/) that Princeton "does not need to waste lots of time and money rewriting Princeton's Zoning to slow or stop tear downs," proposing instead an upfront sliding-scale construction fee for added square footage. The letter illustrates the active debate in the community over *how* — not just *whether* — to act.

- **June (Letter to Editor / Planet Princeton):** A resident [writing in support of the Council initiative](https://planetprinceton.com/2016/06/06/update-on-princeton-residential-zoningteardowns-task-force/) warned that "the avalanche of huge (and often ugly) McMansions **directly threatens the very qualities that have made Princeton so attractive for so long**," citing specifically the threat to 90 wild acres on Herrontown Road.

- **March:** The Witherspoon-Jackson Historic District designation was approved by Council — in part as a defensive measure. [Town Topics noted](http://www.towntopics.com/wordpress/2016/03/09/witherspoon-designation-could-be-decided-on-thursday-night/) that historic designation "comes with restrictions about renovations, improvements, and teardowns," protecting Princeton's historically African-American neighborhood from the same market forces.

- **December:** The Neighborhood Character and Zoning Initiative issued its first recommendations on setbacks, porches, and cathedral ceilings. Council voted to approve an ordinance at its final meeting of the year, with more changes promised for 2017.

## 2017: Official Findings — Problem Has "Increased in Recent Years"

Princeton's Planning Board adopted its [2017 Reexamination Report](https://www.princetonnj.gov/DocumentCenter/View/841/Reexamination-Report-PDF) on November 2, 2017. It is the most direct official statement of the problem's severity to that date.

Under the **Land Use** section, the report states:

> *"Princeton's primary focus has been on the impact of the demolition of residential structures and the replacement of small- or modest-sized homes with much larger homes. **This problem appears to have increased in recent years.** Princeton has enacted new zoning to begin to address this concern and a neighborhood study has been completed which provides a blueprint for zoning fixes to address/protect neighborhood character."*

Under **Housing**, the report adds:

> *"The Community identified the increasing number of teardowns occurring in certain neighborhoods as a growing problem. As indicated under the land use section, these teardowns both affect the visible character of our neighborhoods and impact affordability in these neighborhoods. Small to modest size homes are being demolished and larger more expensive homes are replacing them. The 2007 Reexamination recommended developing new zoning standards to address neighborhood character."*

Notably, the 2017 report also flags the **affordability dimension**: the new larger home "often sells for two or three times more than the demolished home," pricing middle-income buyers out of neighborhoods they had previously been able to afford.

Meanwhile, [Princeton Community News reported](https://www.communitynews.org/archives/barskys-world-making-dream-homes-a-reality-for-some-nightmares-for-others/article_db02245f-9000-51ef-ba55-d2d4d3729de1.html) from the field in 2018 that on "street after street in Princeton, neighbors have stood by helplessly as older homes have been razed and replaced by outsized, overwrought McMansions. In most cases the new homes do not even require a zoning variance."

## 2018: A New Ordinance — and Continued Frustration

### The Ordinance

In October 2018, after two years of workshops, studies, and public input, Princeton Council voted to introduce a new residential zoning ordinance. [Town Topics reported](https://www.towntopics.com/wordpress/2018/10/31/new-ordinance-approved-by-council-addresses-teardowns-zoning-standards/) that consultant Jim Constantine of LRK Inc. reviewed the ordinance, acknowledging that "Princeton certainly isn't the only community dealing with teardowns — it is occurring in many communities across the nation."

The ordinance addressed height, prominent garages, and receding front doors. Mayor Liz Lempert said: *"We've experienced a period where, from the street, a lot of the newer homes seem designed more for cars than for people going in and out."*

[Council voted unanimously in November 2018](http://www.towntopics.com/wordpress/2018/11/21/council-votes-for-ordinance-with-new-zoning-standards/) to pass the ordinance, with a significant limitation: teardowns on **conforming lots** meeting all other bulk requirements were not required to obtain variance relief — meaning many spec builders could continue operating as of right.

### The Continuing Debate in Letters

**August 2018 (Letter to Editor):** A resident [wrote in *Town Topics*](https://www.towntopics.com/wordpress/2018/08/01/time-to-see-past-composting-energy-use-bike-lanes-to-the-elephant-on-the-block/) that she had found a *New York Times* article about Princeton's teardown problem dated 2005 and was exasperated: *"The reality is that plenty of citizens have voiced concerns to the Council over the years (and, yes, are doing so presently), but nothing gets done."* She also called out what she saw as the municipality's hypocrisy in promoting environmental sustainability while enabling the construction of larger, more energy-intensive homes.

**November 2018 (Letter to Editor, Planet Princeton):** Two residents — Walter R. Bliss Jr. and Virginia Kerr — [urgently warned](https://planetprinceton.com/2018/11/17/proposed-zoning-ordinance-revision-will-encourage-teardowns-in-princeton/) that a proposed revision to the zoning ordinance would "clear the way for redevelopment of undersized lots without zoning board review" and would have "the unintended consequence of encouraging teardowns." They argued: *"Any teardown on an undersized lot should be subject to board review in advance and in tandem with analysis of building plans for the replacement structure."*

### The 2018 Barsky Profile and the Hawthorne Avenue Case

Princeton Community News ran an extended profile of RB Homes in 2018: ["Barskys' World: Making Dream Homes a Reality for Some, Nightmares for Others"](https://www.communitynews.org/archives/barskys-world-making-dream-homes-a-reality-for-some-nightmares-for-others/article_db02245f-9000-51ef-ba55-d2d4d3729de1.html). The title captures the community's ambivalence.

The article documents a then-current controversy on **Hawthorne Avenue**, just off Harrison Street, where RB Homes had purchased a 1950s Cape Cod, demolished it, and was constructing a much larger replacement. Neighbor Galina Chernaya discovered that excavation had damaged the roots of five century-old maple trees straddling her property line. She retained attorney Roger Martindell and brought her case to the Shade Tree Commission. A Princeton Tree Care evaluation found: *"Proper root pruning techniques should have been implemented to minimize root trauma."* Chernaya remarked that dealing with regulations not followed made her feel as though she were back in the Soviet Union — though with the difference, she noted, of freedom of speech.

By 2018, RB Homes was operating at scale: **25 homes either refurbished or newly built in a single year, 15 of them in Princeton**. The firm's targets were explicit: Roman and Daniel Barsky described their buyers as "New York urban transplants" and pharmaceutical executives — "young, mid-30s, looking to start or raise their families" — who "want downtown living" and "zero maintenance both on the house and the yard."

## 2021: Still "A New Teardown at Every Corner"

Three years after the passage of the 2018 ordinance, residents were still writing to express frustration. [One letter in *Town Topics* in June 2021](https://www.towntopics.com/2021/06/02/asking-university-to-reconsider-new-east-campus-design-proposal/) — focused primarily on Princeton University's East Campus project — noted in passing: *"Not everything that is new is better. And lately, it seems there is a new teardown at every corner."* The writer argued that Princeton "would not be as famous as it is, nor attract so many visitors, without protection and preservation of our architectural heritage," and invoked the New York Times' own 2021 headline: *"Princeton, N.J.: Historic Homes and Cultural Riches."*

---

## 2022: Ongoing Zoning Battles

In late 2022, the debate over protecting non-conforming lots from teardown pressure continued. [Residents Bliss and Kerr returned to *Planet Princeton*](https://planetprinceton.com/2018/11/17/proposed-zoning-ordinance-revision-will-encourage-teardowns-in-princeton/) to reiterate their objections to zoning changes they feared would make it easier to build on undersized lots — arguing that long-standing doctrines against "self-created hardship" should not be abandoned.

---

## 2023: The Master Plan Confirms the Trend Is Unbroken

The [2023 Princeton Master Plan and Reexamination Report](https://www.princetonnj.gov/DocumentCenter/View/15706/Master-Plan-90-Reexamination-Report-PDF), adopted by the Planning Board on November 30, 2023, confirms what residents had been saying for years: the 2018 ordinance was insufficient. The Report's Land Use section states:

> *"Residential tear-downs and replacement of smaller homes with larger ones continued to be of major concern. The 2017 Reexamination Report recommended additional zoning standards to minimize the adverse effects of this trend.*
> *▶ This continues to be a concern, as evidenced by the drop in the percentage of homes with three bedrooms in favor of homes with four or more bedrooms. This contrasts with the number of smaller households in the Municipality, which is much greater than the number of smaller housing units available to them."*

The statistical evidence the 2023 Report marshals is telling: Princeton's **households are getting smaller** (more single-person and two-person households), while its **housing units are getting larger** (shift from three-bedroom to four-plus-bedroom homes). The teardown-and-upsize dynamic is, in effect, building the wrong housing for the actual population — pricing out exactly the modest-income, smaller-household residents the community has repeatedly said it wants to retain.

---

## 2024: The Market Remains Hot

As recently as September 2024, [Princeton Perspectives reported](https://princetonperspectives.com/new-construction-is-going-up-all-around-town/) that teardown properties "don't last long once listed and often sell above asking price," frequently purchased by builders who construct on spec. Despite more than a decade of ordinances, task forces, and public engagement, the economic fundamentals — land worth far more than the structure on it — continue to drive the market.
