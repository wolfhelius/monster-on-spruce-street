# The Missing Middle and First Homes

### Is Princeton's Affordable Housing Strategy Solving the Right Thing?

*Princeton is meeting its housing numbers. But a number is not a strategy—and the homes behind the number may be the wrong ones for the people who need them most.*

---

## The question worth asking

Princeton has done the hard, unglamorous work of adopting a Fourth Round Housing Element and Fair Share Plan, defending it in court, and committing to build hundreds of deed-restricted affordable units between now and 2035. That is real, and it deserves credit.

But meeting an obligation measured in *units* answers only one question—**how many?**—and leaves two harder ones untouched: **for whom?** and **what kind?** A plan can satisfy every legal requirement by producing small rental apartments, and still fail the families who face the deepest shortage and the would-be first-time buyers who can no longer get a foothold here. The risk, put plainly, is that Princeton is building apartments when what's missing is *homes*.

This piece asks whether the strategy is aimed at the right target. It draws on the national evidence about what new housing actually does to affordability, on Princeton's own demographic data, and on what the town is actually building.

---

## Building more housing matters. The real question for Princeton is *what* to build.

The mainstream position in housing debates—and it has strong evidence behind it—is that the cure for high prices is to build more. The New York Times Editorial Board made that case forcefully in May 2026, showing that the typical American home now costs about five times the median household's income, up from 2.5 times in 1950. The gap tracks construction: San Francisco built only about 22 homes per 1,000 households over the past decade and carries a price-to-income ratio of 12.4, while Austin built roughly 140 per 1,000, sits at 4.6, and has seen home prices fall about 13 percent. The board judged the link between prices and construction "even stronger than many Americans realize," and backed supply that works through filtering—build more of anything, including luxury units, and higher-income residents vacate older housing, easing prices below. The Pew Charitable Trusts reaches a compatible conclusion: more supply means slower rent growth, with the biggest relief in older, cheaper units. Supply is necessary, and Princeton—like the rest of high-cost New Jersey—should build.

This piece does not dispute any of that. It accepts it, and then asks the question a unit count cannot answer: **what kind of housing, and for whom?**

Two things hold even on the pro-supply case's own terms. First, filtering works on *price*, not on *bedrooms*. Higher-income households moving into new studios and one-bedrooms free up other studios and one-bedrooms; nothing in that process turns a glut of small units into homes a family of four can live in. If the shortage is in family-sized homes—and the evidence below says it is—then more small units, however many, leave it open. Second, the same editorial board that wants more building wants a *particular kind*: two weeks later it endorsed Massachusetts' Legalize Starter Homes initiative, framing the goal as family-scale, middle-class, ownership homes that young families can afford and build wealth in. That is the missing middle properly understood—not a stack of 600-square-foot rentals.

So the question for Princeton is not *whether* to build—it should—but whether its strategy produces the homes the need and the demographics actually call for. The rest of this piece argues it does not.

---

## Princeton's own numbers point to families

Princeton's plan justifies a tilt toward smaller units by citing a "mismatch": most households are small (71.4% have three people or fewer), while only 37.3% of homes are efficiencies, one-, or two-bedrooms. That is true as far as it goes. But the plan's own data tells the other half of the story, and it points the opposite direction:

- The number of households with **four or more people grew by almost 50%** between 2013 and 2023, and now make up **28.6% of all households**—more than a quarter of the town (Fourth Round HEFSP, Table 13).
- About **a third of Princeton households include children under 18** (roughly 3,200 of 9,002 households), and family households are two-thirds of the total (Table 14).
- The average household is 2.58 people—and the fastest-growing category is not singles but large households.

In other words, Princeton is not simply shrinking into one- and two-person households. It is also, increasingly, a town of families. Meanwhile its existing stock is large but unaffordable: about 63% of homes have three or more bedrooms, and the median owner-occupied home was worth roughly **$1.04 million in 2023**, up nearly 45% in a decade (Tables 6 and 7). The family-sized homes exist; they are simply priced out of reach.

A strategy that reads "households are getting smaller, so build small" misreads its own evidence. The demographic trend and the affordability problem both point toward family-sized homes that ordinary families can afford.

---

## The gap is family-sized, affordable homes

The national research is unambiguous about where the affordable shortage bites hardest. The Harvard Joint Center for Housing Studies, measuring the supply of family-friendly rentals for households with children, found the deepest deficits among the lowest-income families and identified **two-bedroom homes as the single largest shortfall**, with a gap on the order of 1.4 million units nationally. Yet new construction runs the other way: analyses of recent building (for example, the Bay Area Equity Atlas) show a majority of rentals built since 2010 were studios or one-bedrooms, with three-or-more-bedroom apartments making up only about 15%—even as overcrowding rose among low-income families.

Princeton's recent additions fit the size pattern. The roughly 1,500 units the town added over the past decade were overwhelmingly apartments, and the median one-bedroom now rents for about $2,751. Even granting that added supply eases rents over time, a decade of small-unit construction did little for families, who need bedrooms a one-bedroom cannot provide. The lesson is not that building fails—it is that building *small* does not answer a *family* shortage.

---

## What Princeton is actually building

Set the need beside the plan. Princeton projects as many as **920 new dwellings by 2035**, the large majority of them market-rate, produced mainly through an inclusionary model that requires roughly 20% of units to be deed-restricted affordable. And the plan leaves the *size* of those homes almost entirely to chance:

- The implementing zoning **defers bedroom mix to a state rule (UHAC) that can be satisfied in the aggregate**, so no individual project must demonstrate a family mix.
- The roughly 80% of units that are market-rate carry **no bedroom requirement at all.**
- Even the three sites the city owns and will fund as 100% affordable *family* rental—the Chestnut Street firehouse, the Harrison Street firehouse and garage, and the 303 John Street depot, **85 family units in all**—commit to no family bedroom mix. The only explicit Fourth Round bedroom promise in the entire 272-page plan is that 16 of 33 very-low-income units will have two or more bedrooms.

The emblem of where this leads is already on the table at 86–88 and 92–94 Spruce Street, where the approved AH-12 ordinance permits about 42 units on two-thirds of an acre—roughly 64 units per acre—as predominantly 600-square-foot apartments, with 17 parking spaces and no usable open space. That is a lawful plan. It is also, unmistakably, apartments—not family homes.

---

## "First homes": the dimension the strategy mostly skips

There is a second target the plan largely misses: ownership. Princeton's Fourth Round leans heavily toward rental. Rental affordable housing is essential and the town should build it—but a strategy built almost entirely on small rentals does little to help families build the equity and stability that come from owning a first home.

This is precisely the gap that the "missing middle" and "starter home" movements are trying to close elsewhere. Massachusetts is the clearest example. Its voter-driven *Legalize Starter Homes* initiative—which the New York Times Editorial Board endorsed on May 30, 2026 as a way to let young families and the middle class afford homes—would set a statewide 5,000-square-foot minimum lot in place of the 20,000-square-foot lots some towns now require, enabling more modest single-family homes. Its **Chapter 40Y Starter Home program**—whose implementing regulations were finalized in March 2026—lets towns create districts for "smaller, more affordable" single-family homes aimed explicitly at first-time buyers, downsizing seniors, and smaller households, with rules governing **home size, number of bedrooms, and affordability**, plus open-space requirements. These tools are calibrated to families and first homes by design. Princeton's overlays, by contrast, are calibrated to unit counts and defer the question of bedrooms.

Note the irony: the "missing middle" Princeton's own Master Plan invokes describes house-scale homes—duplexes, townhouses, small ownership-friendly buildings—that families can grow into. What the Fourth Round is delivering is closer to small rental apartments. Same phrase; different product.

---

## Solving the right thing

The fair-share obligation is a floor, not a strategy. Princeton can clear that floor—has cleared it—by building a sufficient *number* of affordable units, while still:

- building for a household profile (small renters) that its own data shows is **not** the fastest-growing one (families);
- aiming at the wrong end of the documented affordability gap (small units, not family-sized ones); and
- under-investing in the first-home ownership that turns a roof into a foothold.

That is what it means to solve the wrong thing well. The honest counterargument—that any new supply helps—is partly right, and this piece does not dispute that Princeton should grow. But in a market this desirable, new small market-rate apartments will not reliably reach the families with the greatest need, and counting doors will not tell us whether we housed them.

A strategy aimed at the right target would measure success differently: by the number of **homes a family can actually live in and afford**, by a real share of **family-sized units** across both affordable and market-rate construction (starting with the 85 units on city-owned land, where the town has total control), and by genuine **paths to ownership**, not only rentals. Princeton has proven it can hit a number. The next test is whether it can house the families—and the first-time buyers—that number was supposed to be for.
